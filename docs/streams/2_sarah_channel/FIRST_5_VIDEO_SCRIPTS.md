## VIDEO 1: Pilates for Beginners — Your First 20 Minutes

**Format:** YouTube long-form (20 min)
**Goal:** Rank for "pilates for beginners" — be the most human, accessible version of this video on YouTube

---

**FILMING NOTES**
- Location: Living room. Real furniture visible in background — this is the point. Don't clear the room too much.
- Wear: Comfortable leggings, a fitted top so form is visible. Not brand-new looking — something you actually wear.
- Mat: Your own mat on the living room floor. Facing front.
- Lighting: Natural light from window, ideally morning. No ring light (too influencer). If you need fill — a standing lamp slightly behind and to the side.
- Camera: Tripod at mid-height, slightly wide so full body is visible. Do NOT film from above.
- Who films: Tripod. No need for Ben on this one — you're teaching, not being documented.
- Props: Nothing needed. Maybe a cushion under the head if you want to mention the modification.

---

**SCRIPT**

[INTRO — no music, just start talking]

Hi. I'm Sarah. And if you're here, you've probably been thinking about starting Pilates for a while — and just not quite done it yet.

Maybe you feel like you need to be more flexible first. Or that it's a bit posh. Or that you'll look stupid because everyone else will know what they're doing.

I'm going to promise you three things before we start.

One — you don't need to be flexible. Especially not for this.

Two — you cannot do it wrong if you're following along with me. I'll give you modifications for everything.

Three — by the end of twenty minutes, you will feel different. I don't mean "transformed." I mean your spine will feel longer, your back will feel a bit more awake, and you'll have done something genuinely good for your body.

That's it. That's the promise. Let's go.

[pause — come to the mat]

I want you to start by lying on your back. Knees bent, feet flat on the floor — hip-width apart. Arms by your sides. Let your head go heavy.

[demonstrate, stay in position and talk]

Take a breath in through your nose — let your belly rise, let your ribs expand. And breathe out through your mouth.

Again. In through the nose — feel your ribcage widen. Out through the mouth — feel everything soften.

We're going to do that three more times. And I want you to actually do it with me — not just watch.

[pause, breathe together visibly]

Good. That's the foundation of everything we're going to do today.

In Pilates, the breath is not a warm-up. It's not something you do before the real bit starts. It IS the real bit. The way you breathe drives your deep core muscles — the ones that support your spine. So when you breathe well, your body works better. Right from the start.

Now — I want to check something. Can you feel the back of your head touching the floor? If your chin is pointing up at the ceiling, use a folded towel or a small cushion under your head. We want the back of the neck long, the chin slightly tucked. Not chin-to-chest. Just neutral.

[demonstrate neutral head position]

That's it. Good.

---

**PELVIC TILTS**

Now — hands flat on the floor beside you, or on your hip bones if you want to feel what's happening.

I want you to gently press your lower back into the floor. Not hard — just a gentle press. Like you're trying to close a tiny gap between your spine and the floor. Hold it — breathe in — breathe out and release. Let the lower back return to neutral.

[do 5 slow reps]

We call this a pelvic tilt. It's activating the deep core — the muscles that wrap around your spine like a corset. Not your six-pack. The deep stuff. These are the muscles that actually protect your back.

Five more. In through the nose, tilt, breathe out, release.

[continue, coaching through breath]

If you feel this in your lower abs — brilliant. That's exactly right. If you feel nothing — that's also fine. These muscles are quiet. We're just waking them up.

---

**KNEE ROLLS**

Arms out wide — a T shape. Let your arms be heavy. Feet still flat on the floor.

On your exhale, let both knees drop slowly to the right. Only go as far as feels comfortable — you don't have to touch the floor. Breathe in at the bottom. Exhale, bring them back to centre.

Other side. Exhale — drop. Inhale at the bottom. Exhale — return.

[do 6 reps, slow, coaching through]

This is a spinal rotation. Your thoracic spine — the upper and mid back — doesn't rotate very much in daily life. Especially if you sit at a desk. This is one of the most effective ways to give it the movement it needs.

Keep your shoulders heavy. Let the rotation come from the spine, not from lifting.

---

**CAT-COW**

Come onto all fours. Hands under your shoulders, knees under your hips. Think of a table — flat back, neutral spine.

[demonstrate the all-fours position carefully]

Breathe in — let your back dip, your chest open, your head lift gently. This is cow.

Breathe out — round everything. Chin to chest, spine to the ceiling, tummy drawing in. This is cat.

[do 8 slow reps, coaching]

These are not big movements. Small, controlled, connected to the breath.

If your wrists hurt — make fists and rest on the knuckles instead. Or come onto your forearms. Both are completely fine.

[continue, more reps, settle into the movement]

---

[Continue through: child's pose, single knee hinge, modified bridge, prone arm reach, roll-down finish]

[For each exercise: demonstrate clearly, name the muscles, give one modification, coach the breath]

---

**OUTRO**

[sitting up on mat, relaxed, genuine]

And that's your twenty minutes. Or thereabouts.

How does your back feel? Genuinely — notice whether it feels any different to when you started. Longer. A bit more awake. Maybe a tiny bit sore in a way that's clearly not damage.

This is what Pilates does. Not dramatically. Not overnight. Over time.

If you want to do this again tomorrow — do it again tomorrow. This same video. Three times a week and you'll feel a real difference in a month. I'm serious.

I've got more videos coming — a whole postnatal series, a back pain series, a whole series on the road when we depart for our four-year expedition next year. If you subscribe, you'll get all of it.

Questions — put them in the comments. I read them all.

See you in the next one.

[wave, smile — not a performance wave, just a real one]

## VIDEO 2: Why I Started Pilates (And Why It Kept Me Sane)

**Format:** YouTube long-form (12–15 min) — talking to camera, personal story
**Goal:** Build trust, establish Sarah's story, rank for "personal Pilates story" / "pilates for back pain"

---

**FILMING NOTES**
- Location: Kitchen table or sofa — somewhere lived-in. Mug of tea visible. This is a conversation, not a presentation.
- Wear: What you'd actually wear on a Saturday morning. Jumper. Normal.
- Lighting: Natural window light. Cloudy day is fine — it's more honest.
- Camera: Tripod, slightly lower than eye level. Mid-shot (head to just below chest). You're talking to the camera as a person.
- Ben: Could be present off-camera, but you're talking to the lens.
- No mat needed. This is a story video.

---

**SCRIPT**

[INTRO — at the kitchen table, mug of tea, settled]

So this is the story video. The one where I tell you why I'm here, why I started a Pilates channel, and what the plan is.

I'll warn you upfront — it involves a back injury, two pregnancies, a four-year road trip, and a decision to retrain as a Pilates instructor at 35. So strap in.

[pause, small smile]

We bought this truck about three years ago. The plan was always — travel. Long-haul overland. Ben, me, the two boys. At the time it felt like a fantasy we were making slowly real. And then my back gave out and it started to feel like something that might actually never happen.

During my second pregnancy — my second boy, Reuben, who is now eight and absolutely exhausting in the best possible way — I developed serious pelvic girdle pain. And then after he was born, I couldn't shift this low-grade chronic backache. Not horrific. Just always there. Sitting was uncomfortable. Picking him up was uncomfortable. Getting out of the car was uncomfortable.

I saw a physio who gave me exercises I didn't understand, a consultant who suggested I consider surgery at some point down the line, and about three people who told me to just do some yoga and take ibuprofen.

I did not feel listened to.

[pause]

What actually changed things was stumbling into a Pilates class at the local leisure centre. Nothing fancy. An evening class, a teacher called Mandy, and me lying on the floor in a sports centre that smelled of chlorine.

The first class — I didn't feel much. The second class — I noticed that my back didn't ache as much driving home. The sixth class — Mandy showed me what my pelvic floor was actually doing (not what I thought it was doing) and something clicked.

Six weeks later, I'd stopped reaching for ibuprofen as a default.

Six months later, the back pain was largely gone.

A year later, I'd decided I wanted to train as an instructor.

[pause, look directly to camera]

Not because I want to be an influencer. Genuinely, not that. But because there are so many women going through exactly what I went through — a body that's changed after babies, pain that nobody takes seriously, an exercise world that doesn't speak to them — and I know what changed it for me. I want to be that for other people.

So I enrolled in APPI Level 3. That's the Applied Physiology and Pilates Institute course — it's physiotherapy-led, which means it's evidence-based, which matters to me. It's a 16-week course. I'm currently [X weeks in].

[pause]

The other thing is — this channel is also going to be about the expedition. Because we're actually doing it. We are leaving [departure date] and we are driving. From here. North. Then south. Then east. Then wherever the road goes.

And I'm doing Pilates the whole way. On the truck. In car parks. On campsites. On ferry decks at 6am.

Because the thing that kept me sane, kept my back functioning, kept me feeling like myself through two pregnancies and a global pandemic — I'm not leaving it behind. I'm taking it everywhere.

[pause — lean back slightly, relax]

This channel is for you if you're a mum who's running out of time. If your back hurts. If you've thought about Pilates and not started. If you're curious about a very specific kind of life — full, noisy, always moving.

I'm not going to be perfect. I'm going to film things in my living room and my kitchen and a Norwegian layby.

But I'm going to be honest. And I'm going to actually know what I'm talking about — because I'm doing the work to earn that.

Subscribe if that sounds like something you want. Questions in the comments. I'll answer them.

See you next Monday.

## VIDEO 3: Postnatal Pilates — What's Safe, What's Not, Where to Start

**Format:** YouTube long-form (18 min) — instructional with personal element
**Goal:** Primary search target: "postnatal pilates" / "pilates after pregnancy"

---

**FILMING NOTES**
- Location: Living room, mat on floor. This is a teaching video.
- Wear: Teaching clothes — fitted top, leggings. Clean but not pristine.
- Camera: Wide enough to see full body for demonstrations. Tripod.
- Lighting: Morning natural light preferred.
- Props: A cushion for head support demonstration. Nothing else.
- Note: Add a clear on-screen card at the start: "This is educational content, not medical advice. Always check with your GP or women's health physio before starting exercise postnatally."

---

**SCRIPT**

[INTRO — standing, not on mat yet]

If you're postnatal and you've been told "you can exercise now" — brilliant. But I want to talk to you about what that actually means. Because "cleared to exercise" is not the same as "ready for a HIIT class."

And I don't say that to be scary. I say it because six weeks postnatally, your body is doing some very specific things that matter for how you move.

Let me explain. And then I'll show you a really solid place to start.

[move to mat, sit]

---

**First — the honest bit**

Your pelvic floor has been through it. Regardless of whether you had a vaginal birth or a c-section — pregnancy puts enormous sustained load on the pelvic floor for 9 months. Labour is an additional event.

Your abdominal wall — the linea alba, that connective tissue down the middle of your stomach — has stretched. For some of you, it's stretched significantly. That's diastasis recti. I've got a whole separate video on that, but the key thing to know is: doing crunches right now is not helping it.

Your hormones, especially if you're breastfeeding, include elevated relaxin — which means your joints are more mobile than they were pre-pregnancy. This sounds like a benefit. It's not. More mobile means less stable. You need to build control before you build load.

So here's the order. And this is the order I'd follow regardless of fitness level, regardless of whether this was your first or your fourth:

**Breath first. Pelvic floor second. Deep core third. Everything else after that.**

I'm going to walk you through the first stage now.

---

**Part 1: Breathing (5 minutes)**

[lie on mat]

Come onto your back, knees bent, feet flat. One hand on your chest, one on your belly.

Breathe in through the nose. Where does your breath go? If your chest rises and your belly barely moves — that's shallow, thoracic breathing. Not wrong, but not where we want it yet.

I want you to breathe into your belly first, then your ribs. Feel your belly rise under your hand, then feel your ribs widen. Not forced. Not a big puff. Just letting the breath go low.

Breathe out slowly through the mouth. Feel everything soften.

[demonstrate 5 breaths]

This is called diaphragmatic breathing. And it's the foundation of postnatal rehab because the diaphragm, the pelvic floor, and the transversus abdominis all work together as a pressure system. When you breathe properly, the whole system starts to coordinate.

We're going to do this for two minutes. I'll do it with you.

[two minutes of coached breathing]

---

**Part 2: Pelvic Floor Activation**

Still on your back. Now I'm going to ask you to find your pelvic floor.

This is the bit that sounds weird but matters so much.

Imagine you're trying to gently stop the flow of wee. The little lift you feel — that's the front part of the pelvic floor. Now add the back — imagine you're trying to stop wind.

The combined feeling — lift and squeeze inward and up — that's a pelvic floor contraction.

Hold it. Not gripping. Not holding your breath. Just a gentle sustained lift. Three seconds. Release. Fully. The release is as important as the lift.

[5 slow reps, coaching through]

Now add the breath. Breathe in — as you breathe out, lift and hold. As you breathe in again, release. The breath and the pelvic floor work together.

[5 more reps]

---

**Part 3: First Gentle Moves**

[single knee fold — demonstrate carefully]
[pelvic tilt series]
[modified bridge — feet flat, small lift, no more]
[heel slide]

For each:
- Name the exercise
- Show the safe version
- Give the "if this doesn't feel right" modification
- State clearly what NOT to do (dome, strain, hold breath)

---

**OUTRO**

[sitting up on mat]

That's where to start. Breath, pelvic floor, and those five gentle moves.

Not twenty minutes of exercise yet. Not a class. Just these things, done carefully, with attention.

In six to eight weeks of doing this daily, you'll be ready for more. I'll have that video coming too.

The most important thing I can tell you is: if anything feels wrong — doming at your midline, pressure downward in your pelvis, leaking, pain — stop. Those are signals. Not something to push through.

There's a free checklist in the link in the bio — the postnatal return to exercise guide. Download it. Take it to your GP appointment.

Subscribe for more of this. And I'll see you next week.

## VIDEO 4: Pilates for Back Pain — The 30-Minute Session I'd Give My Mum

**Format:** YouTube long-form (30 min) — full instructional session
**Goal:** Rank for "pilates for back pain" / "pilates back pain relief"

---

**FILMING NOTES**
- Location: Living room mat. Clear shot of full body.
- Wear: Fitted teaching clothes.
- Props: Nothing — this session is mat-only.
- Filming: Tripod, wide shot. Ben could help with close-up angles if available.
- Medical caveat card at start: "This is educational Pilates content. It is not a substitute for medical advice. If you have a diagnosed condition, please check with your GP or physiotherapist before following along."
- Tone: Warm, patient, slower pace. Your mum is a 60-year-old woman who's had a bad back for 20 years. Film as if speaking directly to her.

---

**SCRIPT**

[INTRO — at mat, seated]

My mum has had a bad back for as long as I can remember. Not diagnosed anything specific. Just — chronic, grumbling, limiting her life in small ways.

She asked me once what she should do. And I've thought a lot about what I'd actually design for her if I could. So this session is for her. And for anyone else in that position — a back that just aches, that limits things, that you've kind of learned to live with.

I want to say upfront — I'm not diagnosing anything. I'm a Pilates instructor in training, not a physiotherapist. If you have disc problems, stenosis, spondylolisthesis, sciatica — please check with your GP before doing this. These exercises are appropriate for general back pain and muscle tension. They're not appropriate for every back condition.

If you've had that check and you're good to go — follow along. Go at your own pace. Never go through pain.

Let's start.

[come to lying down on mat]

---

**PHASE 1: Breath and Settle (5 minutes)**

[Breathing — lying on back]
We start every session with breath. Always.

In through the nose — belly rises, ribs widen.
Out through the mouth — everything softens.

When we breathe like this, the deep core muscles — the ones that wrap around the spine — start to activate. Gently. Without straining. This is the foundation.

Five breaths. I'll do them with you.

[pause, breathe together]

Notice your lower back. Is it lifted off the floor? Is there a gap? That's normal. We're not going to force it flat. We're going to let it find its own neutral.

---

**PHASE 2: Mobilisation (10 minutes)**

Pelvic tilts — slow, small, intentional. 10 reps.

Knee rolls — both knees drop side to side. 8 reps.

Single knee to chest — hold, breathe, release. 30 seconds each side.

Both knees to chest — small circles, slow rock.

Cat-cow on all fours — 10 slow reps. If wrists hurt: on fists, or on forearms.

Thread the needle — rotating one arm under the body, opening the chest. 5 each side.

[for each: full demo, talk through the why, give modifications]

---

**PHASE 3: Gentle Strengthening (10 minutes)**

Modified bridge — feet flat, small lift, hold 5 seconds, lower slowly. 8 reps.

Clam — side-lying, feet stacked, top knee opens. 10 reps each side.

Side-lying leg lift — small, controlled. 10 reps each side.

Heel slide — lying on back, slide one heel out and back while maintaining spinal neutral. 10 reps each side.

[for each: safety cues, breath, alignment]

---

**PHASE 4: Wind-Down (5 minutes)**

Child's pose — 90 seconds.

Lying spinal twist — gentle, held, both sides.

Final breathing sequence.

---

**OUTRO**

[lying on back, looking up]

[pause — genuinely]

How's your back?

I ask people this and they always say "a bit sore." Which sounds bad but it's usually not. It's the muscles waking up. Specifically the stabiliser muscles that haven't been asked to work in a while.

If it feels like that — a deep, muscle ache — that's normal. A hot bath, some water, come back in two days.

If it feels like nerve pain — shooting down the leg, sharp, different to usual — stop and go see your GP.

For most of you, this is the session to come back to three times a week. Same video. Just do it again. Your back will start to feel better within two to three weeks if you're consistent.

My mum is starting this on Monday. I'm going to send her the link and check in with her in a fortnight. If it changes things for her the way it changed things for me — I'll be absolutely made up.

Subscribe. I'll see you next week.

## VIDEO 5: What APPI Pilates Actually Is — And Why I Chose It

**Format:** YouTube long-form (12 min) — talking to camera + some mat demonstration
**Goal:** Educational, trust-building. Rank for "APPI Pilates explained"

---

**FILMING NOTES**
- Location: Kitchen table first (talking), then living room mat (demo). Two setups.
- Wear: Comfortable, normal — this is a chat video that becomes a demo.
- Props: Your APPI course materials visible (not necessarily shown in detail, just present). A notebook.
- Tone: Enthusiastic but grounded. You've genuinely found something you believe in.
- No cards or slides needed — just talking. Real.

---

**SCRIPT**

[INTRO — kitchen table, relaxed]

I want to talk about Pilates training. Specifically, why there are so many types and how I ended up where I did.

If you've searched for Pilates courses, you'll know it gets overwhelming fast. Classical Pilates. Contemporary Pilates. STOTT. Body Control. Polestar. APPI. They all claim to be the best and they all hate each other a little bit.

I'm going to tell you why I picked APPI. Not because I'm dismissing the others — I'm not qualified to do that yet. But because the reasons matter, especially if you're a mum, or you've had injuries, or you want something that's actually going to help people rather than just look impressive on a reformer.

---

**What APPI is**

APPI stands for the Australian Physiotherapy and Pilates Institute. It was founded by physiotherapists — and that's the key thing. The whole system is built on physiotherapy evidence and principles.

What that means practically: it's designed around real bodies. Bodies that have had babies. Bodies that have had back surgery. Bodies that have chronic pain. The exercises are designed to be modified. The whole philosophy is about meeting the person where they are — not demanding they meet you where the exercise is.

Compare that to Classical Pilates, which preserves Joseph Pilates's original exercises pretty faithfully. That's a valid and beautiful tradition. But it's not where I wanted to start. Because the people I want to work with don't usually start on a reformer doing the Hundred. They start on the floor, trying to find their pelvic floor, with a toddler somewhere nearby.

---

**The physiotherapy link**

The anatomy in my APPI course is substantial. We're learning the spine in a way that would make a junior physio nod. We cover the pelvic floor as its own unit — the relationship between the diaphragm, transversus abdominis, pelvic floor, and multifidus. We cover diastasis recti, pelvic girdle pain, common postnatal presentations.

This matters if your clients are coming to you with those issues. And mine will be.

---

**What it's like to study**

[slightly more personal here — a bit of course diary tone]

The practical assessments are where you find out if you actually understand it. You're watching someone move and you have to spot the compensation pattern — the way they rotate their pelvis because they can't stabilise through the hip, the way they hold their breath because nobody told them they didn't have to.

It's harder than I expected. And I mean that as a compliment. I expected a workout. I got an education.

[pause]

I'm going to share more of the journey as I go through. The good bits and the hard bits. The anatomy I had to revise four times. The assessment where I absolutely blanked on the muscles of the pelvic floor and had to start a sentence about three times before I found the word I was looking for.

---

**Quick demo — what APPI mat work actually looks like**

[move to mat]

I'll show you two exercises — one that looks very simple and isn't, and one that people think is advanced but has a really accessible version.

[Simple: pelvic tilt with breath — explain all the layers of it]

This is a pelvic tilt. It looks like nothing. But if you do it right — the breath, the pelvic floor activating on the exhale, the transversus drawing in, the multifidus stabilising — there's a lot happening. This is the APPI approach. Every exercise is a system.

[Modified: roll-up vs crunch]

The roll-up is one of the most misunderstood exercises in Pilates. People try it like a crunch — just pull from the neck. This is the version that builds the sequential spinal control the exercise is actually about.

[demonstrate modification clearly]

The modification uses a band. Takes the neck strain out. Teaches the movement properly.

---

**OUTRO**

[back to kitchen/talking]

That's APPI in brief. I'll do more detailed videos on specific elements as I go through the course.

If you're thinking about training yourself — I'll share everything I can from the experience. It won't be a sales pitch. It'll be the honest version.

Subscribe if you want to follow along. I'll see you next week.
