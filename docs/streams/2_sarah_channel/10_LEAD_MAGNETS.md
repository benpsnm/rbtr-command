## Lead Magnet 1: 5-Minute Morning Mobility for Mums

**Landing page description:** "Do this before you check your phone. Before the kids come downstairs. Five minutes to set your spine up for the day. No mat, no equipment, no faff."

---

### 5-Minute Morning Mobility for Mums
*A Pilates-based sequence you can do in your pyjamas*

**Before you start:** Find a clear bit of floor. You don't need a mat. You don't need to be warm. You just need five minutes.

---

**Move 1: Knees-to-Chest Rock (60 seconds)**
Lie on your back. Bring both knees into your chest. Wrap your hands around your shins. Gently rock side to side — not a stretch, just a release. Let your lower back settle into the floor. Breathe in through the nose, out through the mouth. Do this for 60 seconds.

*Why:* After sleep, your lumbar spine is compressed. This gentle mobilisation creates space and wakes up the nervous system without demand.

---

**Move 2: Knee Rolls (60 seconds)**
Stay on your back. Feet flat on the floor, knees bent. Arms wide like a T. Let both knees fall slowly to one side — only go as far as feels comfortable. Pause. Return. Other side. Don't rush. Your breath pulls you back, not your muscles.

*Why:* Rotates the thoracic spine, releases the obliques, gently activates the deep core.

---

**Move 3: Pelvic Tilts (60 seconds)**
Still on your back, knees bent. Press your lower back gently into the floor (posterior tilt) — hold 2 seconds. Release to neutral. Repeat. There's no "arch." Just gentle, small, intentional movement. This is core activation, not a back exercise.

*Why:* Wakes up the deep stabilisers without loading. Safe for postnatal, back pain, and complete beginners.

---

**Move 4: Cat-Cow (60 seconds)**
Come onto all fours — hands under shoulders, knees under hips. Inhale: let the back dip and head lift (cow). Exhale: round the spine, tuck the chin (cat). Move with the breath, not against it. Do 8–10 rounds.

*Why:* Mobilises every segment of the spine. This one move does more for morning stiffness than almost anything else.

---

**Move 5: Thoracic Opener in Side-Lying (60 seconds each side)**
Lie on your side, knees bent at 90 degrees (stacked). Top arm reaches forward at shoulder height. Inhale. On the exhale, sweep the top arm slowly behind you, opening the chest to the ceiling. Don't force it — go as far as your ribcage lets you. Inhale here. Exhale back. 5 reps each side.

*Why:* The thoracic spine is where most of us carry tension. This gentle opener releases the chest and mid-back without any loading.

---

**Done. That's five minutes.**
You can do this every day. You can do it twice. There's no wrong version.

## Lead Magnet 2: Diastasis Recti Recovery Checklist

**Landing page description:** "Not sure if you have diastasis recti? Not sure what to do about it? This checklist gives you a clear starting point — what to check, what to avoid, and what actually helps."

---

### Diastasis Recti: Your Recovery Checklist
*A practical starting point — not a diagnosis, not a quick fix*

---

**What is diastasis recti?**
Diastasis recti (DR) is a separation of the two halves of the rectus abdominis (your "six-pack" muscle) at the midline. The tissue between them — the linea alba — stretches and sometimes doesn't fully return after pregnancy.

It's more common than you think (up to 60% of women have some degree of it at 6 weeks postpartum). It's not always obvious. And it doesn't always need specialist intervention — but it does need the right movement.

---

**Step 1: Check yourself (the finger test)**

Lie on your back, knees bent. Relax your abdomen. Place two fingers horizontally at your belly button. Gently lift your head (like you're looking at your knees). Feel if your fingers sink in, or if you feel a gap between two ridges of muscle.

- Gap of 1 finger or less: likely no significant DR
- Gap of 2 fingers: mild — follow movement guidelines
- Gap of 3+ fingers: moderate to significant — I'd recommend seeing a women's health physio

*Note: the gap alone isn't everything — tension/depth matters too. A physio can assess this properly.*

---

**Step 2: Check your symptoms**

Tick any that apply:

☐ Doming or coning at the midline when you do a sit-up
☐ Lower back pain that's been persistent since pregnancy
☐ Pelvic floor dysfunction (leaking, heaviness, urgency)
☐ A "gap" you can feel between your abdominal muscles
☐ Feeling like your core "doesn't work" even when you exercise it
☐ Abdominal bloating that seems exercise-related

If you ticked 3 or more, I'd recommend starting with the gentle programme below and seeing a women's health physio before progressing to anything loading.

---

**Step 3: What to stop doing (for now)**

❌ Sit-ups and crunches
❌ Leg raises (both legs straight)
❌ Heavy lifting without breath control
❌ Exercises that cause doming at the midline
❌ High-impact before your pelvic floor is ready
❌ Planks until your deep core is stable enough to hold them

This isn't "never do these things." It's "not yet."

---

**Step 4: What to start doing**

✅ Diaphragmatic breathing — this is the starting point for all DR rehab
✅ Gentle pelvic floor activation (see Lead Magnet 6 in this series)
✅ Transversus abdominis activation (belly button drawing in)
✅ Modified bridge
✅ Clam exercise
✅ Side-lying leg work
✅ Gentle walking

---

**Step 5: The progression principle**

Before you add load, add stability. Before you add stability, add breath. The sequence is:

Breath → pelvic floor → deep core → movement → load

Most postnatal fitness content skips the first three. Don't.

---

**When to get help**

Please see a women's health physiotherapist if:
- Your gap is 3+ fingers
- You have pelvic floor symptoms alongside the DR
- Pain accompanies any of the movements above
- You've been exercising and the gap isn't closing

## Lead Magnet 3: Pilates for Back Pain on Long Drives

**Landing page description:** "You're about to get in a car for four hours. Or you just did. Here's what your spine needs — before, during, and after."

---

### Pilates for Back Pain on Long Drives
*Pre-drive, mid-stop, and post-drive sequences for a happy spine*

---

**Why long drives hurt**

When you sit for extended periods, especially in a car seat:
- Your hip flexors shorten and pull on your lumbar spine
- Your thoracic spine rounds forward, compressing the vertebrae
- Your glutes switch off (literally — they stop firing)
- Your pelvic floor muscles fatigue under sustained load
- Blood pools in the legs

The result: a lower back that feels like concrete when you get out.

---

**Part 1: Pre-Drive (5 minutes before you get in)**

Do these in a car park, on a verge, anywhere:

**Hip flexor stretch:** Kneeling lunge, back knee down. Gently tuck your pelvis (posterior tilt) and breathe into the front of your back hip. 60 seconds each side.

**Thoracic extension:** Find a wall or the side of the car. Place your hands above your head, lean your chest forward gently. Let gravity open the chest. 30 seconds.

**Pelvic floor release:** Stand with feet wide, squat slightly, breathe in and let everything relax downward. This sounds counterintuitive but sustained sitting loads the pelvic floor — releasing before you get in helps.

---

**Part 2: Mid-Drive (at every stop — even just 2 minutes)**

**Seated rotation:** Still in the driver's seat. Hands on the wheel. Rotate your whole torso to the left, look behind you. 5 reps each side. You'll hear things.

**Ankle pumps:** Flex and point your feet 20 times. This keeps circulation going and reduces the ache in the calves.

**Shoulder rolls:** Forward 5, backward 5. Bring your shoulder blades together and release. Breathe.

---

**Part 3: Post-Drive (the first 10 minutes after you park)**

Don't immediately lift anything. Your spine needs to decompress.

**Walk for 2 minutes.** Even just to the service station and back. Let the spine find its neutral again.

**Wall cat-cow:** Hands on the bonnet (it's warm, it's fine). Arch, round, arch, round. 8 reps.

**Standing forward fold:** Feet hip-width, soft knees, let your head and arms hang. Stay 60 seconds. This reverses the compressive load.

---

**Driver vs. Passenger**

Drivers: Your right hip will be more stressed. Give extra attention to the right hip flexor and the right glute.

Passengers: You have more freedom. Use it. Do seated spinal rotations, put your feet on the dash and do figure-4 stretches. Move around.

---

**Long-haul tip**
Every two hours minimum: get out and move for 5 minutes. Set a timer. The drive will take longer but your body will work better when you arrive.

*This sequence is part of our Pilates on the Road series. We drive a lot. This is how we survive.*

## Lead Magnet 4: 7-Day Pelvic Floor Reset

**Landing page description:** "Seven days, 5 minutes a day, to reconnect with and strengthen your pelvic floor. For new mums, experienced mums, and women who've never thought about this before."

---

### 7-Day Pelvic Floor Reset

*A gentle week-long programme for reconnection and strength*

---

**Before Day 1: Find your pelvic floor**

The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles that runs from your pubic bone (front) to your tailbone (back), and sits under your bladder, bowel, and uterus. Most women either can't find it or grip everything at once when they try.

Two ways to find it:

1. **Imagine you're trying to stop mid-wee.** The lift you feel — that's part of it. (Don't actually stop mid-wee; this is just for identification.)

2. **Imagine picking up a blueberry with your vagina.** (I know. But it works better than any clinical description.)

The correct sensation is a gentle lift and squeeze inward and upward. Not a bum clench. Not held breath. Not a full-body grip.

---

**Day 1 — Find and release**
5 minutes. Lying down, knees bent. Try to find the pelvic floor lift (see above). Hold for 3 seconds. Release fully. The release is as important as the lift. 10 reps. Rest. 10 more.

**Day 2 — Breathe into it**
5 minutes. Same position. Breathe in — let your pelvic floor naturally relax and descend. Breathe out — gently lift. Don't force it. Let the breath lead. 10 rounds.

**Day 3 — Long holds**
Today: lift, hold for 10 seconds, release. 5 reps. Rest 30 seconds between each. This builds endurance — important for the moments when you need sustained support (standing in a queue, carrying the shopping).

**Day 4 — Flicks**
Fast lifts and releases. 10 fast, 10 slow, 10 fast. These train the quick-response fibres — the ones responsible for coughing, sneezing, jumping.

**Day 5 — Sitting up**
Same exercises, but seated on a dining chair. This is harder because gravity is working against you. Do Day 2's breathing sequence seated.

**Day 6 — Standing**
Pelvic floor lift while standing, feet hip-width. Hold 5 seconds. Release. 10 reps. This is where it starts to translate into real life.

**Day 7 — Functional integration**
Today, do your pelvic floor lifts during daily activities: when you stand up from a chair, lift the shopping, pick up a child. This is the goal — not separate exercise, but integrated awareness.

---

**Warning signs — please see a women's health physio if:**
- You feel pain during these exercises
- You leak despite doing these exercises for 4+ weeks
- You have a sensation of heaviness or bulging in your pelvic region
- You simply feel disconnected no matter what you try

## Lead Magnet 5: Pilates for Desk Workers — 10-Minute Daily Sequence

**Landing page description:** "Eight hours at a desk is undoing your posture, tightening your hips, and loading your lumbar spine. This 10-minute sequence reverses it. Do it before you shut your laptop."

---

### The Desk Worker's 10-Minute Pilates Reset

---

**What sitting does to your body**

After 8 hours at a desk:
- Hip flexors are shortened (they're been contracted all day)
- Thoracic spine is rounded forward
- Lower back is fatigued from sustained load
- Neck and upper traps are tight
- Glutes have basically forgotten what they do

This sequence targets all five.

---

**The Sequence (10 minutes)**

**1. Thoracic extension over chair back (1 min)**
Sit at the edge of your chair. Fingers laced behind your head. Let your mid-back extend over the top of the chair back. Look at the ceiling. Breathe in. Hold 3 seconds. Come back. 8 reps. If your chair has no back, use a foam roller.

**2. Seated hip flexor stretch (2 min)**
Sit sideways on the chair, right leg off the side. Let the right leg drop toward the floor — gentle hip flexor stretch. Tuck your pelvis (posteriorly) to increase the stretch. 60 seconds each side.

**3. Shoulder blade squeeze and hold (1 min)**
Stand or sit tall. Draw your shoulder blades together and down. Hold 5 seconds. Release. 10 reps. This directly counteracts the rounded forward posture from screen time.

**4. Standing cat-cow (2 min)**
Stand with your hands on the desk, feet hip-width. Same movement as floor cat-cow. Arch, round. 12 reps. Works better in standing for desk workers because it loads the shoulders differently.

**5. Calf raises with pelvic floor (1 min)**
Stand behind your chair, hold for balance. Rise onto your toes — at the top, add a pelvic floor lift. Lower down, release. 12 reps. Gets circulation going and activates the system.

**6. Glute bridge (2 min)**
Lie on the floor (yes, at your desk — it's 90 seconds, you'll survive the looks). Knees bent, feet flat. Press feet into the floor and lift your hips. Hold 5 seconds. Lower. 10 reps. Wakes the glutes up after a day off.

**7. Child's pose (1 min)**
End here. Kneel, sit back on heels, arms stretched forward. Breathe into your lower back for 60 seconds. Let everything go.

---

**Do this every day. Friday afternoon especially.**

## Lead Magnet 6: The Postnatal Return to Exercise Guide

**Landing page description:** "You've been 'cleared' at six weeks. Here's what that actually means — and the sensible order for getting back to exercise after having a baby."

---

### Postnatal Return to Exercise: The Real Guide

---

**The six-week clearance myth**

At six weeks postnatal, your GP will likely ask how you're feeling and sign you off to exercise. What they won't assess: your pelvic floor function, diastasis recti, the state of your c-section scar (if applicable), or your readiness for anything load-bearing.

The six-week check is a starting point, not a green light for HIIT.

---

**What's actually happening in your body at 6 weeks**

- The uterus has mostly returned to its pre-pregnancy size
- Hormone levels (particularly relaxin) are still elevated, especially if breastfeeding — your joints are more lax than normal
- The pelvic floor has been significantly stressed regardless of delivery method
- The abdominal wall is still regaining function
- Your sleep deprivation is affecting your nervous system, proprioception, and injury risk

You are not the same body you were before pregnancy. That's not bad — it just means the return needs to be gradual.

---

**The correct order of postnatal return**

Phase 1 (0–6 weeks): Rest. Gentle breathing. Gentle walking. Nothing more.

Phase 2 (6–12 weeks): Pelvic floor rehabilitation. Breath-led core reconnection. No loading. Walking increasing to 20–30 min daily. Swimming if scar is healed.

Phase 3 (12–16 weeks): Begin gentle loading — bodyweight squats, bridges, modified Pilates. No running. No jumping.

Phase 4 (4–6 months): Low-impact strength. Pilates progression. Cycling. Yoga.

Phase 5 (6+ months): Return to higher-impact activity — running, HIIT, lifting — provided pelvic floor is symptom-free.

This is conservative. It is also correct.

---

**Warning signs to stop and seek help**

- Any leaking (urine, wind, stool) during exercise
- Heaviness or bulging in the perineal area
- Doming at the midline during core exercises
- Pain in the hips, pelvis, or lower back
- Feeling of "falling out" in the pelvic region

If any of these occur: stop the activity, and see a women's health physiotherapist.

## Lead Magnet 7: Pilates for Perimenopause — What Changes and Why

**Landing page description:** "If you're in your late 30s or 40s and feeling like exercise isn't working the same way it used to — you're not imagining it. Here's what's happening and what Pilates can do about it."

---

### Pilates for Perimenopause: The Honest Guide

---

**What perimenopause actually is**

Perimenopause is the transition toward menopause — the phase where oestrogen and progesterone begin to fluctuate and eventually decline. It can start in your late 30s, though most women notice it in their 40s. It can last up to 10 years.

It's not just hot flushes. It's:
- Changes in body composition (more fat, less muscle, especially around the abdomen)
- Decreased bone density
- Changes in joint health
- Altered sleep
- Mood shifts
- Changes to pelvic floor function
- Reduced recovery rate from exercise

If exercise feels harder than it did ten years ago — this is probably part of why.

---

**Why Pilates is particularly good for this phase**

1. **Bone density:** Resistance-based exercise (which Pilates is) stimulates bone formation. Weight-bearing Pilates helps maintain bone mineral density at a time when it's declining.

2. **Muscle mass:** Pilates builds lean muscle, which is metabolically active and supports your metabolism as it shifts with hormonal changes.

3. **Joint health:** The controlled, low-impact nature of Pilates is joint-friendly — important when relaxin fluctuations make joints feel unstable.

4. **Pelvic floor:** Oestrogen decline affects the pelvic floor — tissues become thinner and less elastic. Pelvic floor Pilates directly addresses this.

5. **Nervous system:** Pilates is inherently mindful. The breath-based practice supports the nervous system during a time of hormonal disruption.

---

**Practical adjustments for perimenopausal Pilates practice**

- Train 3–4 days per week rather than daily (recovery time increases)
- Include resistance-based moves, not just stretching
- Prioritise sleep — it affects everything else
- Adjust to your energy levels across the month; they will vary more than before
- Add weight-bearing moves (standing work, modified push-ups)
- Keep pelvic floor work in every session

---

**What to look for in an instructor**

Ask if they have experience with women's health. A good Pilates instructor should be comfortable discussing perimenopause, pelvic floor, and the specific adaptations needed. You should feel seen, not just counted.

## Lead Magnet 8: Travel Wellness — Keeping Your Body Healthy on the Road

**Landing page description:** "Road trips, flights, long drives — movement matters more when you're stuck in a vehicle. Here's the practical guide we use on our four-year overland expedition."

---

### Travel Wellness: Keeping Your Body Moving on the Road

*From someone who actually lives on the road*

---

**Why travel breaks your body**

Extended travel involves:
- Sustained sitting (compression, hip flexor shortening)
- Disrupted sleep (affects recovery, inflammation, mood)
- Dehydration (planes are at 10–15% humidity; most people forget to drink)
- Dietary changes (less fibre, more processed food, irregular meal times)
- Emotional stress (planning, navigation, the unexpected)
- Reduced movement opportunities

Your body is designed to move frequently and variably. Travel removes both.

---

**The Pilates toolkit for travel**

You need: a travel mat (foldable, 2–3mm thick, lightweight). That's it.

**Daily non-negotiables:**

1. Morning breathing (3 minutes): Even in a sleeping bag. Breathe in for 4, out for 6. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system after a night of potentially disrupted sleep.

2. Spine mobilisation (5 minutes): Cat-cow, knee rolls, thoracic opener. Doesn't matter where. Campsite, car park, service station.

3. Walk before driving (10 minutes): Before a long driving day, walk. Don't get straight in the vehicle.

4. Mid-drive stop movement (2 minutes every 2 hours): Hip flexor stretch, calf raises, shoulder rolls. Timer on your phone.

---

**For very small spaces**

All four of these can be done in a 2×1m space:
- Supine breathing and pelvic floor work
- Modified bridge
- Clam (side-lying)
- Thoracic rotation in seated

We do Pilates in the truck. The floor is 1.8m × 1.2m. It works.

---

**Hydration and nutrition for travellers**

- Minimum 2 litres of water per day (more in heat)
- Keep nuts, dried fruit, oatcakes in the vehicle — avoid the service station sugar trap
- Eat a high-protein breakfast when possible (stabilises blood sugar for the day)
- One proper vegetable-based meal per day matters more than you think

## Lead Magnet 9: Back-to-Basics Pilates — The 8 Principles

**Landing page description:** "Before you do a single move, you need to understand why. Here are the 8 principles of Pilates — explained properly, in plain English."

---

### The 8 Principles of Pilates: What They Mean and Why They Matter

---

**1. Concentration**
Every movement in Pilates is intentional. You're not going through the motions — you're connecting your brain to the specific muscles you're working. This is why Pilates is often described as "mindful movement." The quality of attention matters more than the quantity of reps.

**2. Control**
Pilates was originally called "Contrology" by Joseph Pilates. Nothing is done sloppily or with momentum. Slower and smaller is usually harder. The goal is to make the muscles do the work, not the joints and connective tissue.

**3. Centring**
The core — what Pilates calls the "powerhouse" — is the foundation of all movement. This isn't just your abs; it's the deep stabilisers, pelvic floor, multifidus, and transversus abdominis. Every movement begins here.

**4. Breathing**
In Pilates, the breath is not background — it's part of the exercise. Exhalation accompanies effort. Inhalation prepares. This drives the deep core, manages intra-abdominal pressure, and prevents the bracing that injures people.

**5. Precision**
One perfect repetition is worth ten sloppy ones. This is why Pilates classes are smaller sets with more attention. You're not counting reps — you're building neuromuscular patterns.

**6. Flow**
Movements connect. There's a rhythm and continuity to Pilates that distinguishes it from isolated exercises. One move flows into the next. This keeps the nervous system engaged and prevents the compensation patterns that come from stopping and starting.

**7. Imagination**
Many Pilates cues are imagistic — "imagine your spine is a string of pearls," "send your breath into your back ribs." This isn't whimsy. Different cue types access different motor learning pathways. The imaginative cues are often the ones that unlock the movement.

**8. Integration**
Pilates sees the body as a whole system. You can't strengthen the core in isolation — when the core activates, the breath changes, the pelvic floor responds, the shoulders draw back. Integration means you're never working one thing.

---

**Why this matters for you**

Understanding these principles means you'll get more from every session — even with a YouTube video, even without a teacher in the room. You'll know when something feels wrong. You'll know why the breath matters. You'll stop counting reps and start listening to your body.

That shift — from "exercise" to "practice" — is when Pilates changes your life.

## Lead Magnet 10: The Kids' Movement Playbook — Getting Your Children Moving with Pilates Principles

**Landing page description:** "They won't call it Pilates. They won't sit still for it. But these games build body awareness, balance, and coordination — and they'll want to do them again."

---

### The Kids' Movement Playbook
*For parents who want to get their children moving without a fight*

---

**The principle**

Children under 10 don't exercise — they play. The goal here isn't to give them a fitness routine. It's to give them movement experiences that build proprioception, balance, coordination, and body awareness. The Pilates principles (concentration, control, breath) are woven in without being named.

---

**Game 1: The Statue Game (Balance)**
Stand on one leg. Freeze. Who can hold it longest? Add arms out to the side. Then add eyes closed. Then add the other child trying to put them off (gently). Develops single-leg balance, which is foundational for everything from running to climbing.

**Game 2: The Slow-Motion Race (Control)**
Race from one end of the garden to the other. But in slow motion. Arms swinging slowly. Knees lifting slowly. Full foot contact with the ground. The loser is whoever moves fastest. This is harder than it sounds and develops deliberate movement control.

**Game 3: Animal Walks (Strength and coordination)**
Bear crawl (hands and feet, hips high). Crab walk (hands and feet, belly up). Caterpillar (plank walk-in with the feet, walk out with the hands). Seal walk (on hands only, dragging legs). Do a circuit — 10 metres of each. They will think this is hilarious. It's also very hard core work.

**Game 4: The Breathing Game (Breath awareness)**
Breathe in for 4 counts together, breathe out for 6. One round is one point. See who can do ten rounds without forgetting. This teaches breath extension without the word "breathwork" ever being said.

**Game 5: Mirroring (Body awareness)**
Stand facing each other. One leads, one follows — like looking in a mirror. Slow, deliberate movements. Switch leader every 60 seconds. This develops kinesthetic awareness and actually requires deep concentration.

**Game 6: Balance Board / Wobble Cushion Challenges**
If you have a wobble cushion: stand on it. Then stand with eyes closed. Then throw and catch a ball while standing on it. Then squat. Build the challenge. Wobble cushions are worth every penny.

---

**Our experience**

We've been doing versions of these with our boys since they were small. They still do the animal walks at 8 and 10. They have no idea it's exercise. They have extraordinary body awareness and proprioception. It works.

*These games travel well. They need no equipment. They've been played in car parks across Europe.*
