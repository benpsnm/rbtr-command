# Sons Portal — Age-Appropriate Copy Guide

**Purpose:** Language calibration for every content type. Reference this before writing any caption, fact, or voice note.
**Principle:** Neither boy is talked down to. Hudson is treated as genuinely curious. Benson is given what he can actually use at three.

---

## Section 1 — Writing for Hudson (age 7 at expedition start)

### Who he is (for copy purposes)

Hudson can read full sentences without effort. He enjoys facts, especially those involving large numbers, extreme environments, and vehicles. He asks follow-up questions — so a good fact should be rich enough to answer the first one he'll think of.

He responds to "you" language. He is the protagonist of his own story. The copy should treat him that way, not as a passenger being told about his parents' adventure.

He can handle mild tension or difficulty. He doesn't need everything sanitised. "This part will be hard" is exciting information to a seven-year-old, not a warning.

He is literal and precise. If you say three days, he will ask which three. If you say "very big," he will ask how big. Be specific where you can.

### Sentence construction

- Full sentences are fine. Two or three sentences in a block is the sweet spot.
- Complex sentence structures are readable for him — but don't add complexity just to show it.
- Start with the exciting thing, then add the detail. Don't bury the lead.
- Use "you" and "we" — never "the boys" or third-person Hudson.

### Sample copy by section

**Countdown:**
> "Hudson, in [N] days, we leave. The truck is ready. Are you?"

> (When N < 30:)
> "Hudson, there are [N] days left. The bags are nearly packed. The truck is full of fuel. This is really happening."

**Truck Progress — weekly caption:**
> "This week Dad fitted the second leisure battery — that's the one that runs the fridge when we're parked up in the desert. It weighs 14kg and it'll keep things cold for three days without needing sun."

> "The roof rack is on. It took all afternoon to get it level. It's welded solid — strong enough to hold a spare tyre, all the camping gear, and a very small hippo."

> "The solar panels went on today. Six of them. On a clear day they'll make enough power to charge everything we're carrying. We tested them in the warehouse with the lights off and the fridge on. It worked."

**Country fact — Morocco:**
> "Morocco is where the Sahara begins. The Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth — if you drove across it without stopping, it would take three full days. We're going to camp in it."

**Country fact — Senegal:**
> "Senegal is on the far west coast of Africa — so far west that it's one of the first places the sun hits every morning. The national bird is the pelican, which is enormous. Their beaks can hold more fish than their stomach can."

**Country fact — Mauritania:**
> "Mauritania is mostly sand. Almost the whole country is Sahara Desert. The capital city is called Nouakchott, and it was only built in 1958 — it's younger than your grandparents. Before that, nobody thought you'd want to build a city there."

**Packing list item note:**
> "Your headtorch is in. It's got a red light mode — use that when you don't want to wake anyone up in the tent. The white light is for finding things. The red light is for moving without disturbing anyone."

> "This is your penknife. It's yours, not Dad's spare. You'll use it for cutting cord, whittling sticks, and opening things. There are rules, and you already know them."

**Memory Wall — drawing caption:**
> "Hudson's drawing of the truck, March 2026. He got the roof rack right."

**Adventure Log entry (post-departure, sample):**
> "Day 7 — Morocco. We crossed into the desert today. The road stopped being tarmac and became gravel, and then became not much at all. Hudson found a piece of what we think is fossilised shell, 400km from the sea. The Sahara used to be an ocean floor. We didn't know that either."

## Section 2 — Writing for Benson (age 3 at expedition start)

### Who he is (for copy purposes)

Benson is three. He cannot read. He can recognise his own name. He understands images immediately — animals, vehicles, faces, colours. He understands volume and size. He loves things that are big, loud, or funny.

He needs his name in the copy. Hearing (or seeing) his name anchors him. It tells him this is his, not just Hudson's.

One idea per element. Not one idea per paragraph — one idea per card. If the card says "CAMEL" and there's a photo of a camel, that's complete. A second idea is a distraction.

Copy should almost entirely be image-supported. Text is labels. Labels should be three words maximum. One word is often better.

### Sentence construction

- All caps for emphasis words: "BIG." "LOOK!" "BENSON'S." This is how you convey excitement in text to a pre-reader — the shape of the word conveys energy.
- One sentence. Often one word.
- No subordinate clauses. No "which is" or "because."
- His name appears at least once per section.

### Sample copy by section

**Countdown:**
> "Benson! [N] more sleeps!"

> (When N < 30:)
> "NEARLY TIME, Benson!"

**Truck Progress — caption:**
> "LOOK! A BIG ROOF RACK! Shiny!"

> "THE FRIDGE IS IN. Cold things. Benson's snacks."

> "THE TRUCK IS GETTING BIGGER. Dad did it."

**Country — Morocco:**
> "CAMEL. [photo of camel in Moroccan dunes]"

**Country — Senegal:**
> "PELICAN. [photo of pelican on water]"

**Country — Ghana:**
> "DRUM. [photo of traditional drum]"

**Country — The Gambia:**
> "MONKEY. [photo of baboon or colobus monkey]"

**Packing list item:**
> "Benson's sleeping bag. It's BLUE."

> "Benson's headtorch. YELLOW. His."

> "Benson's backpack. RED. He carries it himself."

**Memory Wall — drawing caption:**
> "Benson's drawing. He drew the truck. 2026."

**Voice note transcription (displayed below audio player):**
> "Dad says hi, Benson."

## Section 3 — Voice Note Script Guide

### What a voice note is

Ben at the warehouse, talking to his sons. Real voice, not a podcast. Not polished. The sound of a dad who's been working all day and picks up his phone because he wants his boys to hear this.

The boys will listen to these again when they are older. Benson will not remember being three. These voice notes are partly for now, partly for then.

### Tone

- Warm but not sentimental (Ben is not a sentimental person in presentation — the warmth shows through the content)
- Casual but purposeful — he has something to tell them
- Treats both boys as present, even though they're not there. Addresses them directly.
- Ends with something visual — something they can picture in the truck or the warehouse

### Opening template

> "Alright boys, it's [month], I'm at the warehouse, and you won't believe what happened this week…"

Variations:
> "Hey lads. It's Friday. I've just finished up here and I wanted to tell you something before I drove home."

> "Hudson, Benson — listen to this. You know the [part of the truck]? Well…"

The opening should drop them into the warehouse immediately. No preamble.

### Structure (loose — not a script)

1. **What happened this week** (one thing, specific)
2. **Something funny, weird, or surprising** (optional but preferred — the memorable bit)
3. **What it looks like now** — describe the truck so they can picture it ("If you came to the warehouse right now, you'd see…")
4. **What's coming next** — builds anticipation
5. **Close** — something concrete ("Next time you see the truck, [X] will be done.")

### Length

- Target: 2 minutes
- Acceptable: 90 seconds to 3 minutes
- Below 90 seconds: feels short, like he ran out of things to say
- Above 3 minutes: Benson is gone; Hudson may stay but it's pushing it

### What not to do in a voice note

- Don't say "um, so, basically" in every sentence — not because it's unprofessional, but because it actually makes it harder to follow
- Don't apologise for the background noise. The warehouse sounds are the point.
- Don't say "I know you're probably busy" or "I won't keep you long." They're three and seven. They're never too busy for Dad's voice from the warehouse.
- Don't make it about work stress. The truck is an adventure, not a burden. If it was a hard week, the note is "we hit a snag, but we sorted it, and here's how."

### Example voice note — full transcript (for reference)

> "Alright boys. It's the last Friday of March, I'm standing in the warehouse, it smells like welding flux and I've got dust in my hair. You won't believe what we did this week. The roof rack is on. Properly on — welded, checked, tested. I climbed up on top of it and jumped on it, and it didn't move. That thing will hold everything. Spare tyre, camping gear, all of it. When you next see the truck, it's going to look completely different from the top. It looks like a proper expedition truck now. Next up is the fridge. That'll go in the back left corner, and when we're parked up in the desert and it's 45 degrees outside, that fridge will be running on solar power making everything inside it cold. Benson, that means cold drinks. Hudson, that means the food stays good for days. Alright, I'm going to drive home. See you boys soon. Love you."

## Quick Reference Card

| Context | Hudson | Benson |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Countdown | "Hudson, in [N] days, we leave. The truck is ready. Are you?" | "Benson! [N] more sleeps!" |
| Truck photo | 2 sentences, one embedded fact | 3–5 caps words, max |
| Country fact | 2–3 sentences, specific numbers | One word + photo |
| Packing item | One sentence with a real detail | "[Name]'s [item]. It's [colour/size]." |
| Memory Wall | Normal caption, speaks to him | His name + one descriptor |
| Adventure Log | 3–6 sentences, one photo, one fact, one feeling | Photo 80% of card, one-sentence caption |

## Words That Work

**For both boys:** real, big, strong, your, we, Dad, ours, yet, almost, now, ready

**For Hudson specifically:** because, which means, that's how, actually, exactly, technically (when explained)

**For Benson specifically:** LOOK, BIG, HIS, shiny, loud, fast, gone, there, wow

## Words to Avoid

- "amazing" (overused, meaningless)
- "incredible" (same problem)
- "journey" (too abstract for Benson, slightly cliché for Hudson)
- "experience" (this is a word adults use when they don't know what to say)
- "adventure" used too often (save it — it loses power if it's in every line)
